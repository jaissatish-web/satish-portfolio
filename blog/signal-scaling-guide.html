4–20 mA scaling: from current to a useful process value

Understand live zero, span and reverse scaling with a worked pressure example.

By Satish Kumar Jaiswal · 2026-09-23

4–20 mA ConverterEngineering Unit Converter

A current reading becomes useful only when its configured range is known. A 12 mA signal can mean 50 bar, 200 °C or another midpoint entirely. Before calculating, read the transmitter lower and upper range values and confirm that the receiving system uses the same engineering units.

The span is the difference between the limits

For a linear 4–20 mA signal, the usable current span is 16 mA. Subtract 4 mA, divide by 16 and multiply by the process span. Then add the lower range value. The lower limit does not need to be zero.

Worked example

A transmitter is ranged −50 to 150 °C. At 12 mA, the fraction is (12 − 4) / 16 = 0.5. The indicated temperature is −50 + 0.5 × 200 = 50 °C. At 16 mA, it is 100 °C.

Use reverse scaling to prepare a test

To find the expected current for a known process value, reverse the calculation. A process value of 100 °C in the same range is 75% of span, so the expected output is 4 + 0.75 × 16 = 16 mA. The live chart marks the selected operating point on the straight-line characteristic.

Separate scaling from instrument condition

Four milliamps represents the lower calibrated value, not a loss of power. Currents outside 4–20 mA can reflect overrange, underrange or a configured diagnostic state. Read the device configuration before labelling a particular value as a fault. A scaling calculator does not identify the physical cause of an abnormal current.

A useful verification sequence

  1. Confirm tag, range, unit and linear or square-root mode.
  2. Compare expected values at 0%, 50% and 100%.
  3. Check the transmitter and receiving display against the same range.
  4. Record actual readings and investigate differences before changing configuration.

Pressure-unit conversion preserves the reference basis: converting bar to kPa does not turn gauge pressure into absolute pressure.
