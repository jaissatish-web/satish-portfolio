DP level: why a wet leg can give a negative range

Understand process head, transmitter elevation and the reference-leg offset.

By Satish Kumar Jaiswal · 2026-09-23

Hydrostatic LevelWet & Dry Leg Level

A DP level transmitter measures the pressure on its high-pressure side minus the pressure on its low-pressure side. Liquid height produces hydrostatic head. In a pressure-balanced closed vessel, common vapor pressure cancels from the difference, while the heads in the impulse lines remain.

Define the geometry first

The calculator uses a bottom HP tap and a transmitter mounted below that tap by distance z. The HP line contains process liquid. Zero level is at the bottom tap, and the measuring span is the vertical height above it. A wet LP leg has a constant filled height measured from the transmitter to the reference liquid surface. Dry-leg gas density is neglected.

Worked wet-leg example

Use process density 800 kg/m³, measuring span 4 m, transmitter offset 1 m and a 5 m water wet leg. At zero level, DP = (800 × 1 − 1000 × 5) × 9.80665 / 1000 = −41.1879 kPa. At full level, DP is −9.8067 kPa. The positive span is 31.3813 kPa.

Negative DP does not mean negative level

The reference head can exceed the HP head throughout the measuring range. A correctly configured transmitter can still send 4 mA at empty and 20 mA at full. Move the level slider to see that relationship without confusing pressure zero with level zero.

What changes the answer?

Process density changes the span. Reference-fluid density and fill height change the offset. Loss of wet-leg fill, gas pockets or condensate in a nominally dry leg can therefore alter the indicated level.

Limits of this layout

This model does not cover a transmitter above the lower tap, remote-seal capillary systems, mixed-density HP legs or changing reference level. Sketch the actual elevations and fluid columns before applying a range calculation to another arrangement.
