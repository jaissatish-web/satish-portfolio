Cv and Kv: a useful estimate, not a complete valve selection

Explore the simplified liquid-flow relationship and know when it stops being sufficient.

By Satish Kumar Jaiswal · 2026-09-23

Valve Cv / Kv

A flow coefficient describes a valve’s capacity under defined conditions. Cv and Kv use different reference units. For preliminary liquid calculations, Kv relates flow in m³/h to pressure drop in bar and liquid specific gravity. A coefficient is not itself a valve diameter or an installed opening percentage.

Worked example

For 10 m³/h of water at 1 bar differential pressure, Kv = 10 × √(1 / 1) = 10. Using Kv = 0.865 Cv gives Cv ≈ 11.561.

Explore the pressure-drop curve

Hold the coefficient and density fixed. In the simplified relationship, flow increases with the square root of differential pressure. The chart shows this calculated curve and the selected operating point. Doubling the flow would require four times the pressure drop if the same simplified assumptions remained valid.

Density changes the coefficient requirement

For a given volumetric flow and pressure drop, a higher specific gravity requires a larger coefficient in this model. Enter the liquid property at the relevant operating condition rather than assuming every liquid behaves like water.

Where a preliminary calculation ends

The tool assumes turbulent, non-choked liquid service. It does not evaluate cavitation, flashing, vapor pressure, pressure recovery, attached fittings or viscosity corrections. Gas and steam require different methods. A plotted increase in flow is therefore not a prediction of what a real valve will do after it reaches choking conditions.

Prepare the next engineering step

Collect minimum, normal and maximum flow cases, inlet and outlet pressures, operating temperature and fluid properties. Compare the required coefficients with vendor sizing and the selected valve characteristic. Review controllability, shutoff, materials, noise, actuator requirements and operating range before selecting the assembly.
